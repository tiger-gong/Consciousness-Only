# five faculties

## Explanation（English）
In “five faculties,” faculty (indriya) means that which is able to produce, and also carries the sense of “predominance”—like the root of a plant, able to produce trunk, branch, flower, and fruit. The faculties are further divided into the subtle-form faculty and the coarse-dust (physical) faculty. The coarse faculty, also called the external faculty, is made of the coarse form of the four great elements: the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body—the five sense-organs mutually visible to us. But these external faculties have no capacity to give rise to consciousness; their function is to support the inner faculty, hence they are also called the “supporting-dust” faculty. The subtle-form faculty, also called the inner faculty or the faculty in the ultimate sense, is a transparent, pure kind of matter made of the subtle form of the four great elements. It is the locus where consciousness arises; in terms of modern scientific knowledge, these are the nerve fibers and nerve cells. A Buddhist verse describes the external faculties: “The eye is like a cluster of grapes, the ear like a rolled leaf, the nose like two hanging gourds, the tongue like a new crescent moon, the body like the head of a waist-drum.” The Abhidharmakośa states: “The five faculties are the so-called eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body faculties.” See the entry on the “perceiving-portion.”
