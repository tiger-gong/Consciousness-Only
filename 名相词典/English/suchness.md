# suchness
**梵 / Sanskrit**：*tathatā*

## Explanation（English）
Refers to the true substance pervading the whole universe; the source of all existence, meaning that which is originally and naturally real. It is the true substance pervading the universe—that is, the true appearance or true nature of things. In fascicle 360 of the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-sūtra twelve names are listed—such as dharma-nature, dharma-realm, non-false nature, unchanging nature, nature of equality, nature apart from arising, dharma-determination, dharma-abiding, the real limit, the realm of space, and the inconceivable realm—all synonyms of suchness. Others such as nirvāṇa, the unconditioned, emptiness-nature, the supreme meaning, the one-suchness, and thusness are likewise synonyms. Suchness became the emblem of Mahāyāna Buddhism, the substance of all existence, and on this basis the “one seal of reality” was established, in contrast to the “three Dharma-seals” of Hīnayāna Buddhism. Even so, the schools and masters differ in their interpretation of the meaning of suchness. Ding Fubao's Dictionary of Buddhism explains suchness thus: “The ‘true’ has the sense of the real; the ‘such’ has the sense of the constant. The essential nature of all dharmas, being apart from the false and hence real, is called ‘true’; abiding constantly without change or alteration, it is called ‘such.’” The Dharmasaṃgīti-sūtra says: “Good son, as for suchness—suchness is called emptiness, and that emptiness neither arises nor ceases.” Fascicle 2 of the Cheng weishi lun says: “‘True’ means real, showing it is not false; ‘such’ means constant, indicating it is without change. It means that, being real with respect to all dharmas and ever constant in its nature, it is called suchness.” The “Expedient Means” chapter of the Lotus Sūtra says: “The true mark of all dharmas—namely, that all dharmas are thus in mark, thus in nature, thus in essence, thus in power, thus in function, thus in cause, thus in condition, thus in fruit, thus in retribution, and thus ultimately equal from beginning to end.” Fascicle 8 of the Huayan jing tanxuan ji says: “Indestructible is ‘true’; without difference is ‘such’—the former not shifted by the four marks, the latter without distinction in essence; this is the Elementary Teaching. Again, unchanging is ‘true’; complying with conditions is ‘such’—by the former sense, not identical with conditioned dharmas; by the latter sense, not different from conditioned dharmas; the two senses together being one dharma, it is called suchness.” Thus the Emptiness school takes the empty mark of all dharmas as suchness, while the Existence school takes the perfectly accomplished real nature as suchness; the Tiantai school, on the basis of the doctrine of “nature-inclusion,” holds that suchness itself originally includes all dharmas, defiled and pure, good and evil; the Huayan school, on the basis of the doctrine of “nature-origination,” holds that “the substance is the phenomena”—suchness is originally the myriad dharmas and the myriad dharmas are originally suchness. The latter two were both influenced by the Awakening of Mahāyāna Faith. Originally, the suchness spoken of in Indian Buddhism was basically an objective, static principle-substance, an eternally unchanging truth, as in the Heart Sūtra: “All dharmas are marked with emptiness; they neither arise nor cease, are neither defiled nor pure, neither increase nor decrease.” But in China, after the appearance of the Awakening of Mahāyāna Faith, which set forth “one mind opening into two gates,” it was said: “The so-called non-arising and non-ceasing unite with arising-and-ceasing, neither identical nor different”; and again: “The pure dharma of suchness is truly without defilement, but because it is perfumed by ignorance, there appear defiled marks.…” Thus suchness became not only an objective, static principle-substance but also a subjective, dynamic functioning—becoming “that which neither arises nor ceases, apart from the marks of speech and of names, yet able to give rise to the substance of the myriad dharmas.” Suchness being able to give rise to the myriad dharmas would then differ little from the self-nature or divine Brahman of the outer paths. If we examine the meaning of suchness from the Buddha's basic doctrine of “dependent arising,” then dependent arising means that all dharmas arise dependent on conditions, arising through mutual relation and dependence. The Saṃyukta-āgama (sūtra 298) says: “Because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises; because this does not exist, that does not exist; because this ceases, that ceases.” This means that the arising and existence of any dharma must have its mutually related factors and conditions. Since it arises through mutual relation and dependence, it is understandable that it has no substance of its own, no independent self-nature. Being without substance or self-nature is precisely emptiness—emptiness-nature; this is the essence of all dharmas, originally so, neither increasing nor decreasing, neither arising nor ceasing, the absolute principle-substance—this is suchness. For example, “all formations are impermanent”—the principle that these impermanent formations are by nature originally empty is suchness; “all dharmas are without self”—the principle that this self and these dharmas are by nature originally empty is likewise suchness; extending this, the myriad dharmas all bear this meaning. Therefore suchness is the intrinsic principle of the myriad dharmas, neither identical with nor different from them, and is their true nature. That this principle is “neither identical with nor different from” the myriad dharmas means: because this principle of original emptiness of self-nature resides within the arising and ceasing of the myriad dharmas, and apart from arising-and-ceasing dharmas there is no such suchness, it is “not different”; yet arising-and-ceasing dharmas are impermanent, while this principle of impermanence is constant, so it is “not identical.” The principle that all dharmas are by nature originally empty is the real principle of all dharmas; their emptiness-nature and empty mark are also the real nature and real mark of all dharmas. Hence suchness is the real nature of all dharmas, the nature free of error.
