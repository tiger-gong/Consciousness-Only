# dependent origination
**梵 / Sanskrit**：*pratītyasamutpāda*

## Explanation（English）
Dependent origination (Skt. pratītya-samutpāda) means “arising through mutual relation.” This is a fundamental doctrine of Buddhism and the Buddha's basic view of life and existence. The Buddha held that in the phenomenal world there is nothing that exists eternally and nothing that exists in isolation; all things arise and exist through mutual relation and dependence. Thus the Saṃyukta-āgama (sūtra 293) explains dependent origination: “Because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises; because this does not exist, that does not exist; because this ceases, that ceases.” That is, all arising and existence come about through mutual relation. This relational dependence is of two kinds: one is the relation of simultaneous co-dependence manifested in space; the other is the relation of cause and effect across different times, manifested in time. The cause-and-effect relation across time is the so-called “because this arises, that arises; because this ceases, that ceases”—this being cause and that being effect. The simultaneous relation of co-dependence is the so-called “because this exists, that exists; because this does not exist, that does not exist”—this being primary and that being subordinate. Yet this causal and primary-subordinate ordering is not absolute; viewed from another angle, cause and effect, primary and subordinate, can be reversed. Hence the sūtra says: “The Buddha said: it is like two sheaves of reeds that can stand only by leaning on each other. Friends, conditioned by name-and-form there is consciousness; conditioned by consciousness there is name-and-form; when this arises, that arises; when this ceases, that ceases—just so. Friends, of two sheaves of reeds, remove this one and that one cannot stand; remove that one and this one cannot stand. When name-and-form ceases, consciousness ceases; when consciousness ceases, name-and-form ceases—just so.” Some say that “dependent origination” (older translation: causes-and-conditions) and “dependent arising” (缘生) may be used interchangeably; in fact there remains a distinction: dependent origination is named from the side of the cause, while dependent arising is named from the side of the effect. Liangbi's commentary says: “As for ‘dependent arising,’ ‘condition’ means the manifold conditions, and ‘arising’ means coming forth; all conditioned dharmas arise from conditions.”
